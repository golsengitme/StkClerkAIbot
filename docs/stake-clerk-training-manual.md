Overview for the Stake Clerk
↑ Back to Contents
Purpose  Introduce the stake clerk’s role, the way the stake presidency’s support staff works together, and where to find each training module.
Who this is for: A newly called stake clerk or assistant stake clerk, executive secretaries, and anyone else supporting the stake presidency who wants a reference.

Role and Responsibility
The stake clerk’s duties are documented in the General Handbook: 6.4.2 (Stake Clerk and Assistant Stake Clerks), 29.3 (stake meetings, including 29.3.1 Stake Conference), and 33.3.2–33.3.3 (the stake clerk’s record-keeping, review of ward records, audits and stake history). Many other chapters touch the role too, and each module below links to the sections that apply.
These modules don’t repeat the handbook or the Leader and Clerk Resources (LCR) help pages. They record how our stake actually does the work, so the next person, or a ward clerk looking for help, doesn’t have to start from scratch.
Stake President Direction
Build on a solid understanding of the handbook, then pay close attention to the direction of the stake president and his counselors. Each presidency brings its own approach to administering the stake, and the clerk’s work should fit that approach.
A Supportive Role
A bishop once summed up the role of clerks and executive secretaries in two parts:
	•	Lighten the load. Take on the assignments that can be delegated so leaders can focus on what only they can do.
	•	Make them look good. Leaders are imperfect like everyone else. When we quietly take care of things behind the scenes, they can focus on their core responsibilities without distractions.
Collaboration
Serving the stake presidency is a great blessing. With several clerks and executive secretaries called, more hands mean less individual effort, but it also makes coordination more important. Be clear about who owns each task, so work isn’t left undone or done twice. That unity strengthens everyone who supports the stake presidency and stake leaders.
Several responsibilities have moved over the years. The executive secretaries now lead stake conference logistics and the yearly master file, the assistant executive secretary leads Christmas cards, and the stake technology specialists run conference broadcasts. Where a module describes a supporting role, the clerk’s job is to make the owner’s work easier.
Spiritual Direction
Take time regularly to ponder which areas need your attention. Study the handbook often so the Spirit can prompt you about what to bring to the stake presidency for discussion.
Occasional Assignments
Boundary changes
The stake presidency may ask the stake clerk to use the online boundary leadership tool in LCR (Unit Information › Boundary Proposals) to model ward boundary changes or the creation of a new unit. The stake president submits the final proposal. It happens rarely and takes some time to learn, so allow extra time. Policy is in General Handbook chapter 36; the process is in the Meetinghouse Master Planning Guidelines.
Reporting to the stake presidency
Several recurring items are worth bringing to the stake presidency: quarterly report status and trends, leadership tenure, and the biannual membership audit (see Quarterly Reporting for the Stake Clerk and see Biannual Membership Audit for the Stake Clerk).

Words of Wisdom
These come from serving as a clerk under four bishoprics and three stake presidencies:
	•	Learn how each leader likes to lead, communicate and decide, and shape your support around it.
	•	Timing matters more than it looks. A priesthood ordination recorded on Sunday night can be what lets a young man book his temple trip on Monday.
	•	Assume good intent. When a report is late, someone most likely forgot to press Submit.
	•	Keep nothing sensitive where it doesn’t belong. Templates go in shared folders; real membership council information never does.
	•	Write things down. Most of this work happens once a quarter or once a year, which is just long enough to forget the steps.

Ward Clerk Training
↑ Back to Contents
Purpose  An outline for sitting down with a newly called ward clerk.
Who this is for: The stake clerk, or anyone training ward clerks, and ward clerks themselves.

Background
This outline is based on serving as a ward clerk under four bishoprics and three stake presidencies.
Mission Statement
We help the bishopric by doing the routine tasks that can be delegated to the clerking staff to lighten their load, and by quietly “making the bishopric look good.”
Guiding Values
Effective clerking needs a close partnership with the executive secretary and alignment with the bishop’s leadership style. Learn how the bishop prefers to lead, communicate and make decisions, and shape your support to strengthen his approach. Clear roles, regular communication and coordination with the executive secretary keep work from being duplicated, get tasks done promptly, and give the bishopric unified, reliable support.
A Stake Calling with a Ward Assignment
Ward clerks and ward executive secretaries are recommended by the bishopric, approved by the stake presidency with the high council, and set apart by the stake president, an assigned counselor or a high councilor (General Handbook 30.8.1). Many don’t realize why: these callings connect the ward to the stake.
The clerk’s mission is centered in the ward, but in temporal matters the clerk also represents the stake on the ground. That is the context for quarterly reports, the biannual membership audit and other requests from the stake. The high councilor assigned to the ward plays a similar connecting role.
Financials
	•	Support the bishop and the financial clerk so that processes are understood and follow the audit framework.
	•	Serve as backup when needed.
Membership
Numbers matter
	•	Make sure attendance is being taken as expected (sacrament meeting, classes).
	•	Some measures directly affect the budget the ward receives from Church headquarters.
People matter
	•	Knowing who is attending helps leaders focus ministering.
	•	Move out the records of people who no longer live in the ward.
	•	Request the records of people who live in the ward, especially in apartments.
	•	Make sure ordinances and other information are recorded correctly.

Purpose  Explain how the stake presidency’s shared Google Drive is organized, who owns it, and how to keep access and content clean.
Who this is for: Stake clerks, executive secretaries and members of the stake presidency who create or share content on the drive.

The Drive and Who Owns It
The stake presidency’s shared drive, West Jordan CopperHills Stake, stores and shares files. It is managed by the clerks and executive secretaries, but many people use it, including members of the stake presidency, stake leaders and the technology team.
The drive is owned by design by the stake clerk Gmail account (wjutchstakeclerk@gmail.com), not by any person. Callings change; the account stays. In the past, folders owned by personal accounts caused problems when those people were released. Content should be created in, or moved to, the shared account’s ownership.

Permissions
Access follows the calling. A stake technology specialist who serves as an assistant clerk can see more folders and use LCR features such as sending messages; other technology specialists can’t.
The yearly master file
A new master file used to be created each year. Now the executive secretaries copy or rename the existing file so that nobody loses access and has to start over. That is why, for example, the 2026 master file currently sits in the 2023 folder.
Nobody is doing anything wrong, but keeping one file means that people who have been released can still open it. Some have mentioned that they still can. The content isn’t sensitive (mostly coordination), but access should still be cleaned up.
Periodic Review
Recommended cadence  Review the drive at least twice a year (a good time is alongside the biannual membership audit) and whenever stake callings change.

	•	Open each top-level folder’s Share settings and remove anyone who has been released.
	•	Check the master file’s sharing list in particular.
	•	Look for anything sensitive that has been saved by mistake and remove it.
	•	Move files owned by personal accounts into the shared account’s ownership.
	•	Archive or delete old files that are no longer needed.
Meeting Agendas and Minutes
The printed agenda is still used in meetings, and the clerk adds small notes during the meeting (for example, names when asked to pray). Formal minutes are now produced with ClerkBot; see Meeting Minutes for the Stake Clerk.

Meeting Minutes for the Stake Clerk
↑ Back to Contents
Purpose  Explain how stake meeting minutes are produced with ClerkBot: from the agenda, live notes and recording to clerk-approved minutes. Includes the safeguards that protect meeting content.
Who this is for: Stake clerks and executive secretaries who take, prepare or review minutes.

Principles for AI-Assisted Minutes
Use AI to accelerate capture, transcription, comparison, and drafting while keeping the clerk responsible for the accuracy, confidentiality, and approval of the official record.
AI drafts; the clerk approves. Never treat AI-generated notes as the official record without review.
Keep the source evidence. Use the agenda, recording or transcript, live notes, and authoritative written records to verify important details.
Never let AI guess silently. Verify or flag uncertain names, dates, numbers, decisions, assignments, and conflicting accounts for the clerk.
Use the least-sensitive source necessary. Protect meeting content and do not send recordings to transcription, speaker-identification, or other AI services that are not approved for that content.
Know when not to record. Membership councils are never recorded.
Retain the official record, not unnecessary working data. After the minutes are finalized, delete recordings and transcripts according to the established process.
Human judgment remains authoritative. AI may assist with the work, but the clerk remains responsible for the final minutes.
Where the Instructions Live
The authoritative instructions are in the ClerkBot repository, especially its SKILL.md file. This module summarizes them; if the two ever disagree, ClerkBot wins. The instructions have been shared with the executive secretaries and are stored on the clerk Google Drive: ClerkBot instructions folder.
What It Does
ClerkBot turns a council meeting’s agenda, the live AI notes taken during the meeting (for example, Copilot notes pasted into the agenda) and the recording into a fact-checked, clerk-approved draft of the official minutes. It is used by the clerk or an executive secretary, and produces two files:
	•	Minutes v2.0 (Word): the polished minutes.
	•	Comparison Table (Markdown): every point where the sources were checked against each other, and how each was resolved. It doubles as a record of the clerk’s decisions.
What Goes into the Meeting Folder
Create one folder per meeting, outside the ClerkBot repository (for example, …/Clerking/HC Meeting Source). Put in it:
Source
Role
Agenda (.docx)
The authority for section order and the spelling of names. Opening items (hymn, prayers, prayer list, calendar) are copied straight from it.
Live AI notes (.docx)
Treated as the current draft that gets checked.
Primary recording or transcript
For example, the Teams recording (.mp4) or transcript (.vtt), or an audio file (.m4a).
Backup recording (optional)
A second device, such as a phone voice memo, in case the main recording has gaps.

Safeguards
	•	Audio stays on the clerk’s computer. Transcription and voice identification run locally. Recordings are never sent to a cloud transcription, speaker-identification or other AI service.
	•	Drafting uses Claude Code. The comparison and drafting are done by Claude Code (a hosted AI service) working from the transcript text, within the clerk’s own session.
	•	Nothing is resolved silently. When sources disagree, ClerkBot asks the clerk.
	•	Meeting content stays out of the repository. Deliverables are saved in the meeting folder, and the voice library is never shared or copied off the computer. Only enroll a voice you are sure of.
	•	Names follow the agenda’s spelling. Where a speaker can’t be confirmed, the minutes stay impersonal (“a high councilor reported…”).
	•	Recordings and transcripts are deleted once the minutes are final. Only the reviewed minutes are kept.
	•	Membership councils are never recorded. Use the membership council templates in Clerks › General instead. They are templates only: completed council information is never saved to the drive.
Final Checklist
	•	Every open question has been resolved.
	•	Corrected name spellings no longer appear in their old form.
	•	The Word document has the same number of tables as the draft.
	•	Nothing was sent to a cloud transcription or voice service.
	•	Both deliverables are in the meeting folder.
	•	Any new voice enrollments were confirmed.
Setup (One Time)
ClerkBot needs a Mac with Apple Silicon (M-series), Claude Code, Python 3.9 or later, and ffmpeg. The setup steps, including the local transcription and voice-identification components, are in the ClerkBot README. On other hardware, a different local transcriber must be used; never a cloud transcription service.
Known Issues
	•	In hybrid meetings, Teams may credit every line to one person. Ignore the speaker names in that case.
	•	The transcriber can repeat a phrase over and over during silence or singing. Disregard those passages.
	•	If bullets paste badly into Google Docs, regenerate the Word file with plain bullets (see the README).

Biannual Membership Audit for the Stake Clerk
↑ Back to Contents
Purpose  Help wards complete the twice-yearly membership record audit so records are accurate and leaders can minister to the right people.
Who this is for: The stake clerk, and ward clerks and membership clerks.

What It Is and Isn’t
A membership audit is not like a financial audit. There are no stake auditors and no detailed procedure. Ward leaders review the exceptions summary in LCR, submit the audit, then resolve the exceptions within 30 days. The goal is records in the right state, so leaders are ministering to the people who actually live in the ward.
Deadlines  Twice a year: June 15 and December 15. The stake clerk or an assistant stake clerk makes sure each ward completes it (General Handbook 33.6.19).

Recording in LCR

Record an Ordinance, with pending Melchizedek Priesthood ordinations (names blurred).

	•	Enter the ordination date.
	•	Choose who performed it. When it was the father, select him and his details fill in automatically. Check the last four digits of his membership record number and his birth date against the worksheet.

Recording the ordination. When the father performed it, his details fill in (names blurred).
Why speed matters  Mission calls at 18 compress everything: Melchizedek Priesthood, temple recommend, temple, mission. The temple won’t schedule a brother until his ordination is recorded. Record it the same day if you can.

When a newly called bishopric member needs to be ordained a high priest, the bishop doesn’t need to submit a recommendation first. The stake clerk can open the brother in LCR and choose Print Ordination Form, which fills in his information. This doesn’t start the dashboard workflow; it just saves the bishop an extra step.
Keeping a Copy
The worksheet is just a way to get the ordination recorded correctly. No physical copies are kept. The clerk saves a digital copy on a secured thumb drive kept by the stake clerk, in case the first year brings a question (for example, a certificate that needs reprinting). Remove old files periodically.
